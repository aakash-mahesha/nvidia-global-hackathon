5. Working rhythm — two locations, one clock (EST), evenings

Same timezone removes the handoff tax entirely, so buy speed with synchrony instead of async discipline: the whole team is reachable all day, so nothing should ever be blocked for more than an hour.

  • Fixed window, e.g. 19:00–22:00 EST, five evenings, plus one long weekend block. Pick the hours once and defend them; ad-hoc availability is what kills evening projects. Write the window into the calendar tonight.
  • 10-minute open at 19:00, all four. One agenda item per week — in week 1 it is literally "do the three smoke tests pass yet". Blockers get an owner and a deadline, not a discussion.
  • Last 20 minutes are integration, every evening. Whatever exists gets wired together and run end to end against the mock or the rig. A 3-hour evening cannot absorb a two-week merge, so there must never be one.
  • The weekend block is for the expensive things: rig assembly, data collection marathons, the rehearsal shoot, the edit. Do not spend it on anything that fits in an evening.
  • Always-on room during the window, plus a fixed camera pointed at the rig once it exists. Site 2 must be able to watch a mate attempt without asking.
  • Thirty-minute rule. In a 3-hour evening, stuck for 30 minutes means pull in the second person immediately. Losing an evening to a solo debug costs a sixth of the week.
  • Nightly cron does the work you are asleep for. Fine-tunes and the eval fan-out fire at ~22:30 when you log off; results are waiting the next evening. This is how evenings-only buys back GPU calendar. B1 owns cluster lifecycle: preemptible VMs only, npa cleanup/npa destroy in full order after every session, and a hard kill-timer on every scheduled job — an unattended cluster billing overnight is the one mistake that can end the budget conversation badly.
  • Trunk-based, small PRs, merge the same evening. No branch survives to the next day. CI runs the smoke tests plus a replay of the rig mock.
  • Friday gate, 30 min, all four: hit the week's exit criteria or invoke the cut list. Decisions recorded in CHANGELOG. With this little capacity, cutting on time is the schedule.
  • Swarm the week-3 gate. Same clock means all four can attack the first real end-to-end run together; drop the cloud backlog for that week.

6. One rig, two locations — how site 2 actually touches the hardware

The rig is now shared infrastructure. Treat it like a build server: it needs a network path, a booking protocol, a safety rule and a spares policy. Set all four up in week 1 while it still costs nothing.

Network path (A2, week 1 — before the arm arrives)

  • Tailscale (or equivalent) on the rig host, so both sites reach it by a stable name with no port forwarding. Test it against the rig mock in week 1; do not discover NAT problems on the evening the arm lands.
  • Two always-on video feeds during the window: the wide rig view and a wrist/detail view. Low latency matters more than resolution — site 2 is using these to judge a mate attempt in real time.
  • The event bus is the API. Site 2 drives the rig only through the §3 topics; nobody SSHes in to poke servos ad hoc. Same interface as the mock, so anything that worked in sim works remotely.
  • LeIsaac browser teleop for remote demo recording — this is the feature that makes one rig behave like more than one, because site 2 can record episodes on site 1's arm while site 1 does something else in the room.
  • Auto-upload every episode to Nebius object storage on capture, so data is never trapped on the rig host.

Safety rule — non-negotiable

The arm does not move unless a human at site 1 is physically present with a hand near the E-stop. No unattended remote motion, ever, no matter how late it is or how small the change. The remote operator can command; only the local warden can energise. This is also exactly the story the project is about, so it costs nothing to say out loud in the video.

Booking protocol

  • Rig slots are booked in the shared calendar, minimum 45 minutes, with a named local warden and a named driver. An unbooked evening means the rig is free for whoever is at site 1.
  • Every slot starts by declaring its goal in the channel ("20 demos at fan setting 2" / "verify break-beam abort") and ends with a two-line result. Rig time is the scarcest resource in the project; it should never be spent exploring.
  • Batch the physical changes. A1 keeps a running list of wiring/mounting changes and does them in one sitting rather than one per evening — each teardown costs the whole team a slot.
  • Defaults: hardware-changing work early in the window while both sites are online, data collection later, cron jobs overnight.

Single-point-of-failure policy

  • Spares of everything cheap and breakable, ordered with the first order, not after the first failure: servos (the usual casualty), PSU, USB hub and cables, magnets, a second wrist webcam, extra printed inlets and fiducial rings. A $15 servo that takes five days to arrive can cost you a week of the schedule.
  • The sim stays current all six weeks. It is not scaffolding you throw away in week 3 — it is the fallback that lets four people keep working on the evening the arm is in pieces.
  • Never disassemble the rig after the week-4 rehearsal shoot unless something is broken. Camera positions, lighting and mount geometry stay fixed through filming.

9. Filming with nobody travelling

No travel and one rig means the hardware minute is shot by whoever is standing next to it — A1 and A2 — while B2 directs live over the always-on rig feed. That works, but only if it is treated as a build task with its own parts and its own rehearsal, not as something that happens in week 6.

  • Order the filming kit in week 1, with the rig parts: phone tripod + a second tripod for the wrist/detail angle, a clip-on or USB mic at the rig (servo noise and the magnet click are the money sounds), two cheap LED panels so lighting is repeatable across days, and a long USB/HDMI run so the rig feed does not wobble. Under $150 and it is the difference between one usable minute and four re-shoots.
  • Book the shoot as a long rig slot (§6) across the week-5 weekend block, with the warden committed in advance. It is the single most valuable use of rig time in the project.
  • Two fixed camera positions, marked with tape, so any take on any day cuts together: (1) wide — arm, sprung target, fan, anemometer and the turnaround clock all in frame; (2) tight on the inlet for the align → insert → latch. Never move them after the week-4 rehearsal.
  • B2 directs from the feed in real time during the shoot window; A2 runs the robot, A1 handles the abort beat (the hand entering the workspace) and safety. Nobody at site 1 also has to think about the edit.
  • Screen beats are captured at site 2 — console, permit refusal, the success-vs-wind curve from the nightly run — at full resolution, not filmed off a monitor. They intercut with the hardware footage in the edit.
  • Same-day upload discipline: every take goes to shared storage the day it is shot, with a one-line slate (date, take, wind setting, what it shows). B2 assembles nightly, so by Friday of week 5 you know exactly which beat is missing rather than discovering it during the edit.
  • The rehearsal shoot on 15 Oct is mandatory, even if the robot is only half working. Its only job is to surface the remote-directing problems — audio, framing, feed lag, who says "action" — while they are still cheap.

Consequence of no travel, stated plainly: re-shoots are expensive in coordination, so the schedule buys them back with the week-4 rehearsal and the week-6 slack. If a beat is still missing on Sat 24 Oct, ship the video without it rather than pushing into the 27 Oct freeze.
